# Cocoon: from a short-notice invitation to rehearsing together

Notice at one, arrival requested at two: how did Chen Runze enter rehearsals already under way? Published recollections, programme credits and a performance report distinguish a short-notice invitation, sustained participation and different learning tasks.

Public named recollections read with production, programme and performance records; the individual’s arrival date and training footage remain unmatched · reading-2 · 2026-10-11

## A team short of dancers: notice at one, arrival requested at two

In the backstage account published on 11 November 2020, Chen Runze recalls being invited into Cocoon because the team needed another participant. He initially knew little about the project. Near the end of his account, he describes the urgency: he was notified at one in the afternoon and expected at the venue at two. This records the requested arrival time. The article gives neither the date of that invitation nor his actual arrival time.[[S3]](https://b.p4theater.cn/en/research/cocoon-different-learning-conditions#ref-s3)

Once he joined, accepting an invitation was only the beginning. He says he had to catch up with the group within a short period while also attending to work at school. This was his first experience of this kind of dance theatre and of making a dance with non-professional performers. He describes adapting to different movements and bodies as a process of adjustment. The practical question shifts from finding someone to join to bringing a new participant into rehearsals already under way. That shift is this article’s interpretation of his two passages.[[S2]](https://b.p4theater.cn/en/research/cocoon-different-learning-conditions#ref-s2)[[S3]](https://b.p4theater.cn/en/research/cocoon-different-learning-conditions#ref-s3)

At the end of the interview, he says that after coming to the venue and rehearsing for several days, he no longer regretted participating and found things to learn. His account thus extends from a short-notice invitation to sustained rehearsal. It supplies neither the date of a successful exercise nor an assessment of technical proficiency. The following sections compare his conditions for catching up with Gu Yulan’s separate published account of response and movement memory.[[S3]](https://b.p4theater.cn/en/research/cocoon-different-learning-conditions#ref-s3)[[S1]](https://b.p4theater.cn/en/research/cocoon-different-learning-conditions#ref-s1)

## Responding to another person, and noticing an added response

Discussing contact improvisation, Gu Yulan describes a specific learning task: respond to the force another person applies while noticing whether she has already planned her reaction. She connects bodily rigidity and tension with adding a response of her own. This is her retrospective account of sensations during practice. It directs attention to something beyond producing movement: learning to recognise when a response becomes excessive.[[S1]](https://b.p4theater.cn/en/research/cocoon-different-learning-conditions#ref-s1)

The passage concerns how she understands responding. It is not a diagnosis of a bodily condition, and it does not identify a particular partner or instructor.

## Catching up quickly while adapting to different bodies

Chen Runze locates the difficulty elsewhere: keeping up with the group’s progress within a short time. He describes this as his first encounter with this kind of dance theatre and his first experience completing a dance alongside nonprofessional dancers. Differences in movement and bodies meant that working with different people required adjustment.[[S2]](https://b.p4theater.cn/en/research/cocoon-different-learning-conditions#ref-s2)

Two tasks can be read together here: catching up with work already underway and adapting to different ways of moving. This essay treats them as conditions of participation. It does not turn a personal account into a ranking of ability or assign a teaching role to another participant.

The two accounts can be compared, but they are not two witnesses to the same exercise. Chen does not say that he took part in the contact exercise Gu describes. Neither identifies the other as a partner.[[S1]](https://b.p4theater.cn/en/research/cocoon-different-learning-conditions#ref-s1)[[S2]](https://b.p4theater.cn/en/research/cocoon-different-learning-conditions#ref-s2)

## One recalls a change; the other describes an adjustment in progress

Gu separately discusses learning steps. At first, the flowing movements lacked the memorable points she expected from earlier ways of learning. Later, she found that walking through them repeatedly helped her remember. This is an explicitly recalled change in her assessment of learning. Step memory and contact improvisation are two topics in the interview; they do not establish successive stages of one training programme.[[S1]](https://b.p4theater.cn/en/research/cocoon-different-learning-conditions#ref-s1)

Chen describes working with different bodies as a process of adjustment. He does not specify a trial after which the problem was solved or a sign of completion. Their participation in the same work is therefore no basis for writing the same story of progress for both.[[S2]](https://b.p4theater.cn/en/research/cocoon-different-learning-conditions#ref-s2)

## Shared rehearsal contains different learning tasks

The interpretation proposed here is that rehearsing together contains distinct learning tasks. One person notices whether a response has been decided in advance; another catches up within limited time and adapts to different bodies. Reading the accounts together raises a question about how these tasks coexist. The sources do not establish that one method resolved them at the same moment.

The existing Cocoon essay considers stage choices, viewing positions and other participants’ rehearsal accounts. This reading adds Gu Yulan’s and Chen Runze’s learning conditions; the earlier essay offers a way to continue reading.[[R1]](https://b.p4theater.cn/en/research/cocoon-different-learning-conditions#ref-r1)

The two Backstage articles were published on 13 and 11 November 2020 respectively. Named passages establish the attribution of the accounts used here. Publication dates are not verified practice dates. The materials do not establish a shared class, a partnership, a teacher–student relationship or a conflict between the two people. These passages have not been matched to training footage; the film link below is not matched footage of these exercises.[[S1]](https://b.p4theater.cn/en/research/cocoon-different-learning-conditions#ref-s1)[[S2]](https://b.p4theater.cn/en/research/cocoon-different-learning-conditions#ref-s2) ## Returning an individual’s arrival to the production timeline and credits

A different set of documents supplies the team’s timetable. The production article published on 29 October recalls scheduling the work in September, beginning recruitment in early October and starting rehearsals on 16 October. It describes a production involving nearly twenty people, including work beyond the stage. These are team-level facts. Chen gives no date for his own arrival, so they cannot establish how many days he rehearsed.[[S4]](https://b.p4theater.cn/en/research/cocoon-different-learning-conditions#ref-s4)[[S3]](https://b.p4theater.cn/en/research/cocoon-different-learning-conditions#ref-s3)

The public programme credits Huang Yiyin as director, Hao Jie as choreographer and dancer, and Chen Runze among the dancers. The post-performance report of 5 November confirms the first public performance on the evening of 31 October and separately describes Hao Jie leading contact-improvisation practice. Readers can therefore check the published division of work, a participant’s account and a record of the project reaching performance. The report does not establish that every credited person attended every exercise.[[S5]](https://b.p4theater.cn/en/research/cocoon-different-learning-conditions#ref-s5)[[S6]](https://b.p4theater.cn/en/research/cocoon-different-learning-conditions#ref-s6)

The evidence of an outcome is limited but concrete: Chen says he rehearsed for several days, the programme carries his dancer credit, and the later report confirms the project’s performance. Together they do not make an attendance log or identify him in a particular shot. They add an account of entering shared work to material that previously focused on the difficulty of learning.[[S3]](https://b.p4theater.cn/en/research/cocoon-different-learning-conditions#ref-s3)[[S5]](https://b.p4theater.cn/en/research/cocoon-different-learning-conditions#ref-s5)[[S6]](https://b.p4theater.cn/en/research/cocoon-different-learning-conditions#ref-s6)
