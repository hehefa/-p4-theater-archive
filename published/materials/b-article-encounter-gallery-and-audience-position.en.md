# Meet: who speaks from the gallery, and who watches?

The retained script gives the gallery interruptions; the announcement allows audiences to move. How do these two arrangements enter the text and the space of watching?

Editorial reading of public articles and retained text; see material scope · reading-1 · 2026-10-08

In the retained revised script of Meet, the courtroom gallery does more than watch. It has written responses, interrupts, and affects what the plaintiffs say next. A separate ticket announcement offers audiences freedom to move around and sit on the floor. Reading these materials together distinguishes a speaking task assigned by the script from a mode of watching offered to prospective audiences.[[S4]](https://b.p4theater.cn/en/research/encounter-gallery-and-audience-position#ref-s4)[[S3]](https://b.p4theater.cn/en/research/encounter-gallery-and-audience-position#ref-s3)

Pages 6–7 of the retained seven-page revision contain the courtroom passage. After plaintiffs describe difficulties at work and at home, the gallery turns to professional prestige, income, marriage and outward respectability. Its judgments interrupt the plaintiffs’ accounts; the plaintiffs then respond or reconsider their situations.[[S4]](https://b.p4theater.cn/en/research/encounter-gallery-and-audience-position#ref-s4)

Within the text, the gallery helps drive the dialogue. It brings a person’s account of difficulty into contact with the standards others use to judge that difficulty. This is an analysis of written roles and their exchanges. A character’s dialogue is not a participant’s personal experience.[[S4]](https://b.p4theater.cn/en/research/encounter-gallery-and-audience-position#ref-s4)

The public announcement dated 19 September 2022 describes a different position. Audiences could move freely and sit on the floor, with ten minutes for photographs and conversation after the event. This offers choices about space and a period of post-show exchange. It does not state that audience members would receive the gallery’s lines or could speak into the courtroom scene at any moment. Permission to move does not establish a casting arrangement.[[S3]](https://b.p4theater.cn/en/research/encounter-gallery-and-audience-position#ref-s3)

Two earlier invitations leave that question open as well. The 12 August reading invitation proposes reading first, followed by role recruitment and eventual presentation in a physical space. The 18 August actor call lists music and improvisational interaction among its collaborative strands. These notices preserve plans for joining the project; they do not assign anyone on their lists to the gallery.[[S1]](https://b.p4theater.cn/en/research/encounter-gallery-and-audience-position#ref-s1)[[S2]](https://b.p4theater.cn/en/research/encounter-gallery-and-audience-position#ref-s2)

After the courtroom passage, the revised script returns to a hospital, where the judge also becomes someone being called. This changes the relationship between the written roles again; the text also specifies light and music cues.[[S4]](https://b.p4theater.cn/en/research/encounter-gallery-and-audience-position#ref-s4)

Part of the question “who speaks from the gallery?” therefore still requires evidence from a particular performance. The present materials show more clearly what the gallery does in the text, alongside what the announcement promises about audience space. Keeping these two lines of evidence distinct makes it possible to ask how, and whether, they met on a particular occasion.

## Material scope

This review checked archived public articles and the researcher’s existing text extraction of pages 6–7 of the revision. It did not reopen the original PDF; no script attachment or raw transcript is published. This copy has not been verified as a final script or the paper copy used in a particular performance. Recruitment, audience arrangements and light or music cues are not treated as proof of implementation. Audition footage is not used to reconstruct a finished performance, and the article does not establish the number of speakers, actual casting or participants’ psychological outcomes.
